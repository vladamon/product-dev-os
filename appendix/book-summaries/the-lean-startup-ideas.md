# 13\. The Lean Startup — Eric Ries

**Weight: BACKGROUND — principles only**

For your harness, *The Lean Startup* should function as a **governing philosophy for evidence-driven iteration**, not as an operational playbook. Most of its important ideas are directionally correct but underspecified compared with later frameworks such as *Testing Business Ideas*.

The useful transformation is:

**opportunity → explicit assumption/hypothesis → cheapest credible test → observable evidence → learning → update decision**

## 1\. Validated Learning

**Name:** Validated learning **Status:** Keep — foundational principle

**Explanation**

Progress under uncertainty should be measured by what has been learned about customers, problems, behavior, demand, and the viability of the business—not by how much product has been built.

The important distinction is between:

* activity: interviews conducted, features built, prototypes shipped;
* learning: a belief became materially more or less credible because of evidence.

"Validated" does not mean scientifically proven. It means that the learning is grounded in observable evidence rather than opinion, internal enthusiasm, or customer compliments.

**How it applies to the harness**

Every discovery or validation activity should answer:

What belief are we trying to update?

The harness should not treat an interview, prototype test, landing page, or sales conversation as successful simply because it happened.

It should capture:

* prior belief;
* evidence sought;
* evidence observed;
* whether the evidence supports, weakens, or leaves the belief unresolved;
* what changes as a result.

A useful harness rule is:

No experiment without an intended learning outcome.

**Common misuse**

Calling virtually any customer feedback "validated learning."

Examples:

* "Ten people liked the idea."
* "Users said they would pay."
* "We learned they want dashboards."

Those may be observations, but they are not necessarily strong evidence.

Another misuse is post-hoc learning:

We ran an experiment, saw something interesting, and invented the hypothesis afterward.

**Relationship to Testing Business Ideas**

*The Lean Startup* gives the governing principle.

*Testing Business Ideas* makes it operational by giving:

* explicit assumption mapping;
* testable hypotheses;
* experiment selection;
* evidence strength;
* test cards;
* learning cards;
* experiment patterns.

**Harness treatment:** preserve Ries's principle, implement it using later frameworks.

## 2\. Build-Measure-Learn

**Name:** Build-Measure-Learn feedback loop **Status:** Keep, but reinterpret broadly

**Explanation**

Instead of making long plans and building large products before obtaining feedback, move repeatedly through:

**Build → Measure → Learn**

The deeper point is not "build software quickly."

It is:

Create something capable of generating evidence, observe what happens, and update your beliefs.

"Build" can therefore mean many things:

* interview protocol;
* clickable prototype;
* concierge service;
* fake-door test;
* manual workflow;
* pricing conversation;
* landing page;
* spreadsheet;
* pre-sale offer.

**How it applies to the harness**

Your harness should model validation as loops rather than a linear research phase followed by a build phase.

For each uncertain proposition:

1. identify the uncertainty;
2. determine what observable behavior would reduce it;
3. create the smallest test capable of producing that evidence;
4. observe;
5. update the opportunity/hypothesis/product model.

The harness should maintain traceability:

**evidence → learning → changed belief → next action**

**Common misuse**

Interpreting Build-Measure-Learn as:

Ship something every week.

This easily turns into feature production rather than learning.

Another misuse is beginning with "What can we build?" rather than:

What do we need to learn?

Ries explicitly emphasizes that the loop should effectively be planned backwards from learning.

**Relationship to Testing Business Ideas**

Later frameworks improve this considerably by making experiment design explicit.

For your harness, a stronger formulation is:

**Hypothesis → Evidence requirement → Experiment → Observation → Learning → Decision**

rather than literally starting with "Build."

## 3\. MVP as a Learning Mechanism

**Name:** Minimum Viable Product **Status:** Keep conceptually; replace operationally

**Explanation**

An MVP is the smallest version of an experiment or offering that allows the founder to obtain meaningful learning.

Its purpose is not necessarily to be:

* a small production product;
* version 1 of the final system;
* a low-quality product;
* a subset of the planned feature backlog.

The key question is:

What is the minimum thing necessary to learn whether this assumption is true enough to continue?

**How it applies to the harness**

The harness should never automatically translate a discovered opportunity into:

Build an MVP.

Instead, it should ask:

What is the cheapest credible artifact or intervention that can test the riskiest assumption?

Possible answers may be:

* another interview;
* workflow walkthrough;
* mock-up;
* manual service;
* demo;
* prototype;
* fake-door test;
* paid pilot;
* actual software.

For a solo founder, this distinction is critical. Software development is often a relatively expensive experiment.

**Common misuse**

The worst misuse is:

MVP = shitty first version of the whole product.

Another is:

MVP = the smallest feature list engineers can implement.

Both misunderstand its purpose.

A minimal product that tests nothing important is still waste.

**Relationship to Testing Business Ideas**

Later experimentation frameworks provide much better language because they separate:

* assumption;
* hypothesis;
* experiment;
* prototype;
* test artifact;
* evidence.

For your harness, "experiment" should generally be the canonical concept, while "MVP" remains a historically useful umbrella term.

## 4\. Leap-of-Faith Hypotheses

**Name:** Leap-of-faith assumptions / hypotheses **Status:** Keep, but use stronger assumption taxonomy

**Explanation**

A startup operates on critical beliefs that have not yet been proven.

Ries particularly discusses two major categories:

* **value hypothesis** — whether customers find enough value in the offering;
* **growth hypothesis** — whether adoption can expand sustainably.

The underlying principle is more general:

Identify the assumptions that must be true for the venture to work.

**How it applies to the harness**

After opportunity discovery, the harness should make implicit beliefs explicit.

For example:

Construction managers frequently lose track of field-worker progress.

becomes:

Managers at small construction firms experience sufficiently costly coordination failures that they actively seek or adopt tools to reduce them.

Then decompose further:

* problem exists;
* occurs frequently;
* causes meaningful consequences;
* existing workaround is unsatisfactory;
* decision-maker cares;
* budget exists;
* behavior can change;
* proposed mechanism solves it;
* acquisition is feasible.

The harness should search for **high-importance + low-evidence assumptions**.

**Common misuse**

Writing trivial hypotheses:

We hypothesize that users will like our app.

Or creating hypotheses that cannot meaningfully fail:

Better UX will improve the experience.

Another misuse is testing low-risk assumptions because they are easier.

**Relationship to Testing Business Ideas**

This is one of the clearest areas where Ries should be treated as conceptual background.

Later frameworks are better at:

* assumption mapping;
* desirability/viability/feasibility categorization;
* identifying riskiest assumptions;
* hypothesis formulation;
* experiment matching.

**Harness treatment:** retain the idea that ventures are bundles of assumptions; borrow the operational machinery elsewhere.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 5\. Value Hypothesis

**Name:** Value hypothesis **Status:** Useful, but too broad alone

**Explanation**

The value hypothesis asks whether the product genuinely creates enough value for customers to adopt or use it.

This moves validation away from:

Can we build it?

toward:

Does anyone behave as though this is valuable?

**How it applies to the harness**

Your harness should seek behavioral evidence of value such as:

* repeated workaround;
* significant time/money loss;
* active search for alternatives;
* willingness to switch;
* willingness to provide access/data/time;
* willingness to pilot;
* willingness to pay.

This connects well with:

* *The Mom Test*;
* JTBD;
* switching interviews;
* *Testing Business Ideas*.

**Common misuse**

Treating positive reactions as evidence of value.

Examples:

* "That's cool."
* "I'd definitely use that."
* "Sounds useful."

These are weak signals.

**Relationship to stronger sources**

Ries establishes the category.

Your interview literature provides far better mechanisms for determining whether actual value exists.

This should therefore be represented in the harness as a **validation target**, not an interviewing method.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 6\. Growth Hypothesis

**Name:** Growth hypothesis **Status:** Keep downstream, not early discovery

**Explanation**

A product can create value for a few users without having a scalable mechanism for acquiring more users.

The growth hypothesis concerns how adoption expands.

Ries discusses engines of growth such as:

* sticky;
* viral;
* paid.

For your harness, the specific engine taxonomy matters less than the broader question:

How can this customer population actually be reached repeatedly and economically?

**How it applies to the harness**

Do not emphasize this during early problem discovery.

Once value evidence exists, the harness should begin tracking assumptions about:

* reachable market;
* acquisition channel;
* decision-maker;
* sales cycle;
* CAC implications;
* referrals;
* retention;
* repeatability.

For small-business software this might mean validating whether:

* owner-led outbound works;
* trade associations produce leads;
* referrals occur;
* paid acquisition economics are viable;
* implementation friction kills conversion.

**Common misuse**

Trying to optimize acquisition before validating the problem and value proposition.

The opposite misuse also occurs: treating distribution as something to solve only after the product is finished.

**Relationship to Testing Business Ideas**

Experiment libraries and business-model testing frameworks provide more concrete methods.

Keep growth as a required category of uncertainty, but not as a central discovery-interview concept.

## 7\. Actionable Metrics

**Name:** Actionable metrics **Status:** Keep strongly

**Explanation**

Metrics are useful when they allow you to understand what changed and why, and therefore support a decision.

A good metric should connect reasonably clearly to a hypothesis.

Examples:

* activation rate;
* conversion rate;
* retention by cohort;
* number of users completing a critical behavior;
* pilot-to-paid conversion.

**How it applies to the harness**

Every quantitative experiment should define the measurement **before** running the experiment.

Capture:

* metric;
* baseline;
* target or threshold where appropriate;
* observation window;
* segment/cohort;
* decision implication.

Example:

Instead of:

Measure landing-page traffic.

use:

Among construction-company owners arriving from targeted outreach, what percentage request a demo?

**Common misuse**

Measuring whatever analytics are readily available instead of what relates to the hypothesis.

Another misuse is using a metric without a comparison group, baseline, cohort, or denominator.

**Relationship to Testing Business Ideas**

Later frameworks improve experimental rigor and evidence definitions.

But "actionable versus vanity" remains a genuinely useful filter for your harness.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 8\. Vanity Metrics

**Name:** Vanity metrics **Status:** Keep as an evidence warning

**Explanation**

Vanity metrics make progress appear impressive without reliably supporting decisions.

Typical examples may include:

* total registrations;
* cumulative page views;
* total downloads;
* social followers;
* raw interview counts.

The problem is not that these numbers are always useless.

The problem is that they can grow while hiding whether the underlying business is improving.

**How it applies to the harness**

The harness should challenge any metric by asking:

What decision would change if this number increased or decreased?

If the answer is unclear, the metric is probably weak.

It should also favor:

* cohorts over cumulative totals;
* rates over raw counts;
* behavior over stated interest;
* repeat use over signups;
* revenue behavior over compliments.

**Common misuse**

Calling every top-of-funnel metric a vanity metric.

For example, traffic can be actionable if the hypothesis specifically concerns traffic generation.

A metric is not inherently vain. Its usefulness depends on the hypothesis and decision.

**Relationship to stronger sources**

Later product analytics and experimentation literature offers more precise metric design.

For your harness, this concept works best as a **warning heuristic**:

Evidence must support a decision, not merely create an encouraging number.

## 9\. Innovation Accounting

**Name:** Innovation accounting **Status:** Preserve the logic; do not implement Ries's machinery literally

**Explanation**

Traditional accounting is poorly suited to very early ventures because revenue and profit may not yet provide useful feedback.

Ries proposes measuring progress from an initial baseline, experimenting to improve meaningful metrics, and deciding whether enough progress exists to continue.

The underlying idea is:

Define evidence of progress before certainty or substantial revenue exists.

**How it applies to the harness**

Your harness should maintain an evidence ledger.

For each critical assumption:

* current confidence;
* supporting evidence;
* contradicting evidence;
* source;
* evidence quality;
* latest test;
* movement in belief;
* unresolved questions.

This is essentially a modernized version of innovation accounting.

Example:

**Assumption:** Site managers lose &gt;2 hours/week reconciling worker status.

Evidence:

* Interview A: 3 hours/week reported with concrete example.
* Interview B: no issue.
* Interview C: uses WhatsApp + spreadsheet, \~1 hour/day.
* Direct workflow observation: 35 minutes during one morning.
* Confidence: moderate.

That is much more useful for your harness than a single "validated/unvalidated" label.

**Common misuse**

Building elaborate dashboards before enough evidence exists to justify them.

Another misuse is creating pseudo-precise startup KPIs from tiny sample sizes.

**Relationship to stronger sources**

Later evidence-mapping, assumption-testing, and experiment frameworks are more usable.

Keep the intent:

Make learning progress visible.

Discard most of the original terminology unless useful internally.

## 10\. Pivot or Persevere

**Name:** Pivot versus persevere **Status:** Keep as a decision checkpoint

**Explanation**

After accumulating evidence, the team must decide whether to:

* continue on the current direction;
* substantially change an important assumption.

A pivot preserves some learning while changing another major part of the business model or strategy.

**How it applies to the harness**

The harness should periodically force explicit decisions.

Possible states might include:

* continue investigating;
* strengthen evidence;
* move to solution validation;
* continue current solution;
* modify target segment;
* modify problem framing;
* modify value proposition;
* modify delivery mechanism;
* abandon opportunity.

The key principle is:

Evidence should eventually force decisions.

Discovery should not become endless research.

**Common misuse**

Calling every product iteration a pivot.

Changing a button, feature, pricing detail, or onboarding screen is generally just iteration.

The opposite misuse is refusing to reconsider a major assumption because too much has already been built.

**Relationship to stronger sources**

Later experimentation frameworks give more granular evidence thresholds and decision criteria.

Ries remains useful for insisting that the system must eventually produce an explicit strategic choice.

## 11\. Reducing Batch Size

**Name:** Small batches **Status:** Keep as an experimental design principle

**Explanation**

Large batches delay feedback.

If you:

* design an entire product;
* build it;
* polish it;
* launch it;

before testing assumptions, you may discover months later that the underlying premise was wrong.

Smaller batches shorten the time between assumption and evidence.

**How it applies to the harness**

The harness should encourage decomposition.

Instead of:

Validate the construction-management platform.

break it into assumptions such as:

* daily coordination is painful;
* owner feels the pain;
* owner currently spends time solving it;
* photos/status updates are important;
* workers will use mobile reporting;
* owner will pay €X;
* onboarding can be completed without consultancy.

Each can often be tested independently.

Likewise, interview synthesis should not necessarily wait for 30 interviews.

The system can update its opportunity model continuously.

**Common misuse**

Confusing small batches with doing arbitrary small tasks.

A small task that does not reduce uncertainty creates little value.

The goal is not merely:

Work in smaller chunks.

It is:

Reduce the amount of work exposed to an untested assumption.

**Relationship to stronger sources**

This idea aligns strongly with modern assumption testing.

For your harness, use it as:

Decompose large bets until the riskiest uncertainty can be tested cheaply.

## 12\. Learning Quickly Under Uncertainty

**Name:** Speed of learning **Status:** Keep, with an important qualification

**Explanation**

A startup's scarce resource is not merely engineering capacity. It is runway before discovering whether the business works.

Therefore reducing **time to meaningful learning** is valuable.

The goal is not maximum activity speed.

It is reducing:

assumption → evidence latency.

**How it applies to the harness**

When comparing experiments, the harness should consider:

* learning value;
* cost;
* time;
* evidence strength;
* reversibility.

A two-day manual test that eliminates a fundamental assumption may be superior to a six-week implementation.

You could model this conceptually as:

**experiment efficiency ≈ expected uncertainty reduction / cost and time**

It need not become a literal mathematical score.

**Common misuse**

"Move fast" degenerating into:

* rushed research;
* poor sampling;
* weak experiments;
* shipping before understanding;
* changing direction after one anecdote.

Fast weak evidence is still weak evidence.

**Relationship to Testing Business Ideas**

Later frameworks improve experiment selection by considering cost, duration, evidence quality, and assumptions.

Keep the objective. Use later frameworks for implementation.

## 13\. Measure Before Optimizing

**Name:** Establish a baseline before optimization **Status:** Useful extension of innovation accounting

**Explanation**

Before trying to improve something, establish what current behavior actually looks like.

Otherwise the founder may make changes without knowing whether they improved anything.

**How it applies to the harness**

Before experimenting with a proposed solution, capture the existing workflow:

* current tools;
* time required;
* failure rate;
* frequency;
* monetary consequences;
* workarounds;
* switching behavior.

This is especially valuable in operational B2B discovery.

For example, before asking whether scheduling software improves construction-site coordination, establish:

* how schedules are currently produced;
* how often they change;
* who updates them;
* how many messages/calls occur;
* what happens when information is stale.

That gives later solution tests a baseline.

**Common misuse**

Trying to capture every possible metric before acting.

The baseline only needs to cover the variables relevant to the hypothesis.

**Relationship to stronger sources**

Workflow interviewing, JTBD, and quantitative experimentation provide better operational techniques.

Still worth preserving because it prevents vague "better/worse" judgments.

# What Remains Useful

The enduring Lean Startup ideas for your harness are the meta-rules:

* progress means learning, not output;
* assumptions should be made explicit;
* experiments should reduce uncertainty;
* learning loops should be short;
* products/prototypes are tools for generating evidence;
* metrics should inform decisions;
* evidence should eventually cause continuation, modification, or abandonment;
* unvalidated work should be kept small.

These principles remain strong because they govern **how discovery and validation should behave as a system**.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

# What Is Too Abstract to Implement Directly

Several Lean Startup concepts are insufficient as implementation specifications.

**Validated learning** tells you what you want, but not exactly what qualifies as sufficient evidence.

**MVP** tells you to minimize the artifact required for learning, but does not reliably tell you which experiment to choose.

**Leap-of-faith assumptions** tells you important beliefs exist, but gives a relatively weak taxonomy for finding and prioritizing them.

**Innovation accounting** provides the idea of measuring entrepreneurial progress, but is too broad for a modern AI-assisted evidence system.

**Pivot or persevere** describes the strategic decision but gives limited guidance on evidence thresholds.

These concepts should therefore live mostly in the **principles layer** of your harness.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

# What Later Frameworks Should Operationalize

For your system, the division of labor should roughly be:

| Lean Startup concept  | Better operational source                                 |
| --------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Validated learning    | Testing Business Ideas                                    |
| Hypotheses            | Testing Business Ideas / Lean UX-style assumption testing |
| MVP                   | Experiment libraries and prototype-testing frameworks     |
| Customer evidence     | The Mom Test / Portigal / Deploy Empathy                  |
| Opportunity discovery | Continuous Discovery Habits / JTBD                        |
| Switching evidence    | Demand-Side Sales / JTBD                                  |
| Metrics               | Experimentation + product analytics literature            |
| Pivot/persevere       | Evidence synthesis + explicit decision gates              |
| Small batches         | Assumption decomposition / riskiest-assumption testing    |

The implication for your harness is important:

**Lean Startup should define the philosophy of the learning loop. It should not define the mechanics of interviewing, hypothesis construction, experiment selection, or evidence evaluation.**

Those mechanics are handled substantially better elsewhere.

# Lean Startup Principles Worth Keeping — and Nothing More

For the final harness, I would preserve only these rules:

1. **Treat uncertainty as assumptions that need evidence.**
2. **Measure progress by learning, not product output.**
3. **State what you need to learn before choosing an experiment.**
4. **Use the smallest credible experiment capable of changing a belief.**
5. **Do not equate an MVP with a small production product.**
6. **Prefer behavioral evidence over opinions and enthusiasm.**
7. **Use metrics only when they connect to a hypothesis and decision.**
8. **Reject vanity evidence that cannot materially change a decision.**
9. **Reduce the size of work performed before receiving feedback.**
10. **Optimize for time-to-meaningful-learning, not raw iteration speed.**
11. **Maintain visible evidence for major business assumptions.**
12. **Require accumulated evidence to eventually produce a decision: continue, modify, test further, or abandon.**

Everything beyond that should be delegated to more operational sources—especially *Testing Business Ideas*, your interview-method books, JTBD, and continuous-discovery frameworks.
