The key integration point is this: **Founding Sales should not dominate the earliest research phase.** Kazanjy becomes most useful once you have a plausible problem/ICP hypothesis and need to convert learning into founder-led B2B traction. For the harness, I would let *The Mom Test / Portigal / Hansen / Torres / JTBD* govern evidence collection first, then progressively hand control to *Founding Sales* as commercial evidence accumulates.

# 7\. Founding Sales — Pete Kazanjy

**Weight: CORE FOR THIS USE CASE — exhaustive B2B transition extraction**

## What role this book should play in the harness

*Founding Sales* is primarily about moving from **“we think there is something here”** to **“we can repeatedly find, qualify, sell to, and close the right customers.”**

It is therefore especially strong for:

**ICP → prospecting → qualification → sales discovery → commercial advancement → pipeline → repeatability**

and less authoritative for completely open-ended, pre-solution ethnographic research.

The fundamental founder lesson is:

**Early sales is another form of product learning, but with a much higher evidentiary bar: customers must eventually take costly actions.** [K]

Talking, praising, brainstorming, or expressing interest is insufficient. A founder needs to systematically turn conversations into increasing levels of commercial commitment.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

# I. The six conversational modes

The harness should explicitly maintain a **conversation mode**. Mixing these modes is one of the easiest ways to contaminate evidence.

| Mode                   | Primary question                                        | Commercial intent | Appropriate behavior                                     |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Research interview** | “How does this business/workflow actually work?”        | None              | Explore broadly; don't qualify or pitch                  |
| **Problem discovery**  | “Is this problem real, important, frequent and costly?” | Low               | Investigate behavior, consequences, alternatives         |
| **Sales discovery**    | “Could our solution solve this customer's problem?”     | Explicit          | Investigate fit, impact, stakeholders and buying process |
| **Qualification**      | “Is this an opportunity worth pursuing?”                | Explicit          | Assess fit, pain, urgency, authority and ability to buy  |
| **Pilot discussion**   | “Can we prove value with a bounded engagement?”         | High              | Define scope, commitments, price and success criteria    |
| **Actual selling**     | “Can we reach a commercial agreement?”                  | Full              | Present solution, handle objections, negotiate, close    |

This distinction is partly **[E]**. Kazanjy's book clearly separates prospecting, qualification, discovery, presentation and closing, but the exact research→problem-discovery state machine above is designed for your harness.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

# II. ICP and segmentation

## 1\. Start with a narrow ICP

**Classification:** Principle **Source:** [K]

**Explanation**

Founder-led sales works better when the founder can describe a relatively narrow kind of company for which the product is particularly relevant.

Early founders frequently make their market definition uselessly broad:

“Small businesses.”

or:

“Construction companies.”

Those categories contain radically different workflows, budgets, decision processes and pains.

An ICP should increasingly capture attributes correlated with successful sales.

**Example for a small B2B customer**

Instead of:

Construction companies.

Prefer something approaching:

10–50 employee construction subcontractors with several simultaneous construction sites, one central office, Bauleiter coordinating field staff largely through WhatsApp/phone/email, and no integrated workforce-planning system.

**Observable evidence**

Repeated wins cluster around:

* similar company size;
* similar workflow;
* similar technology stack;
* similar organizational structure;
* similar pain;
* similar buyer;
* similar triggering events.

**What an AI interviewer should infer**

ICP confidence should increase when the same characteristics repeatedly correlate with:

`pain → urgency → engagement → purchase`.

Do not define the ICP merely from people willing to speak with you.

**Appropriate next action**

Prioritize more prospects with the same attributes and test whether conversion remains high.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 2\. Separate account characteristics from buyer persona

**Classification:** Qualification rule **Source:** [K]

**Explanation**

A company can fit the market while the person you are speaking to is the wrong person.

The sales system should distinguish:

**account fit**

from:

**contact fit**.

**Example**

TE-MI Bau might be an excellent account for an operational automation product.

A junior office administrator may nevertheless lack:

* authority;
* organizational context;
* budget influence;
* ability to sponsor implementation.

**Observable evidence**

Account evidence:

* employee count;
* workflow complexity;
* project volume;
* existing tools;
* operational structure.

Persona evidence:

* role;
* responsibility;
* influence;
* ownership of problem;
* purchasing authority.

**AI inference**

Never infer:

wrong contact = wrong account.

**Next action**

Find the person owning the relevant business process or purchase decision.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 3\. Segment before generalizing messaging

**Classification:** Founder-specific heuristic **Source:** [K]

**Explanation**

Early sales messaging should evolve by segment.

A problem that sounds compelling to one industry or company size may be irrelevant to another.

**Example**

“Automatic documentation of worker activity” could mean:

* payroll accuracy to one contractor;
* compliance to another;
* project costing to another;
* customer reporting to another.

**Observable evidence**

Different segments repeatedly react to different value propositions.

**AI inference**

Do not average distinct motivations into vague messaging such as:

“Improve productivity with AI.”

**Next action**

Maintain segment-specific pain and value hypotheses.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

# III. Prospecting

## 4\. Early founders should prospect manually

**Classification:** Founder-specific heuristic **Source:** [K]

**Explanation**

Before building scalable acquisition machinery, founders should directly identify and contact plausible customers.

Manual prospecting creates learning that automated acquisition would hide.

**Example**

Rather than launching broad ads for Feldleiter, identify 50 German construction companies matching the proposed ICP and contact decision-makers directly.

**Observable evidence**

Founder learns:

* who responds;
* which titles matter;
* which message resonates;
* which pain generates meetings.

**AI inference**

Low outbound response may indicate:

* bad targeting;
* wrong persona;
* weak problem framing;
* weak credibility;
* poor message.

It does **not automatically mean the product itself is invalid**.

**Next action**

Diagnose which part of the prospecting funnel is failing.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 5\. Build an explicit prospect universe

**Classification:** Sales process rule **Source:** [K]

**Explanation**

Founder sales should not consist of opportunistically talking to whoever happens to appear.

Create a defined list of target accounts.

**Example**

A founder builds a list of:

* Berlin construction firms;
* 10–60 employees;
* multiple active sites;
* named Geschäftsführer or operations lead.

**Observable evidence**

There is a finite set of identified targets rather than random networking.

**AI inference**

The founder can measure whether an ICP hypothesis produces meetings rather than relying on anecdotes.

**Next action**

Track:

`accounts → contacts → outreach → responses → meetings → qualified opportunities → wins`.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 6\. Personalization matters more early than scale

**Classification:** Founder-specific heuristic **Source:** [K]

**Explanation**

Founder outreach can exploit context and relevance unavailable to generic marketing automation.

Early outreach therefore benefits from being targeted and credible rather than massive.

**Example**

Weak:

We build AI automation for construction companies.

Better:

I noticed you coordinate several Baustellen simultaneously. I'm studying how smaller contractors handle supplier requests and Baustellen reporting when most coordination still happens through email and WhatsApp.

**Observable evidence**

Higher engagement with contextual outreach.

**AI inference**

Specific relevance is more meaningful than email volume at this stage.

**Next action**

Use narrow prospect lists and hypothesis-driven outreach.

# IV. Qualification

Qualification is one of the most important contributions *Founding Sales* makes to your harness.

A founder has limited time.

The system must learn:

**which conversations deserve continued founder effort.**

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 7\. Pain is the foundation of qualification

**Classification:** Qualification rule **Source:** [K]

**Explanation**

An opportunity without meaningful customer pain is weak regardless of how interesting the solution sounds.

**Example**

A Geschäftsführer says:

“Yeah, automatically collecting supplier prices would be cool.”

That is weak.

Much stronger:

“For every tender my Kalkulator spends two days requesting prices from six suppliers.”

**Observable evidence**

Look for:

* repeated problem;
* wasted labor;
* lost revenue;
* delays;
* mistakes;
* risk;
* complaints;
* existing workaround.

**AI inference**

Distinguish:

`interesting capability`

from:

`business problem`.

**Next action**

If pain remains vague, continue problem discovery rather than pitching.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 8\. Quantify the impact where possible

**Classification:** Discovery rule **Source:** [K] + [G]

**Explanation**

B2B pain becomes commercially meaningful when connected to business impact.

**Example**

Not:

Procurement takes a lot of work.

But:

Four tenders a week × three hours of sourcing × €40/hour loaded labor ≈ €1,900/month of staff time.

**Observable evidence**

Customer supplies:

* hours;
* frequency;
* headcount;
* delays;
* errors;
* financial consequences.

**AI inference**

Higher quantified impact generally increases commercial plausibility.

Do not fabricate ROI from weak evidence.

**Next action**

Ask for concrete magnitude and frequency.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 9\. Existing effort is evidence of pain

**Classification:** Buying signal **Source:** [K] + compatible with Mom Test

**Explanation**

Customers already spending resources on a problem provide stronger evidence than customers merely complaining.

Resources can include:

* money;
* employee time;
* consultants;
* manual processes;
* spreadsheets;
* custom tooling.

**Example**

A company pays an employee several hours every week to reconcile construction reports manually.

**Observable evidence**

Actual cost exists today.

**AI inference**

Existing spend establishes a useful baseline for willingness-to-pay reasoning.

**Next action**

Understand exactly what the current solution costs and why they tolerate it.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 10\. Understand the incumbent solution

**Classification:** Discovery rule **Source:** [K]

**Explanation**

Your competitor is often not another startup.

It may be:

* spreadsheets;
* email;
* WhatsApp;
* assistants;
* outsourced labor;
* doing nothing.

**Example**

A construction company may solve workforce coordination using:

WhatsApp + Excel + whiteboard + Bauleiter memory.

**Observable evidence**

Specific tools and procedures are described.

**AI inference**

The incumbent establishes:

* switching costs;
* minimum value threshold;
* hidden requirements;
* actual customer behavior.

**Next action**

Investigate what works well and badly in the incumbent process.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 11\. Urgency is separate from pain

**Classification:** Qualification rule **Source:** [K]

**Explanation**

A company can have severe pain and still not buy.

There must usually be some reason to solve it **now**.

**Example**

Pain exists for years.

Then:

* the company grows;
* an employee resigns;
* a new project doubles workload;
* a major customer demands reporting;
* new compliance requirements appear.

Now urgency changes.

**Observable evidence**

Customer references:

* deadline;
* new contract;
* expansion;
* staffing problem;
* operational crisis;
* budget cycle.

**AI inference**

`pain ≠ purchase timing`.

**Next action**

Ask:

What happens if nothing changes over the next six months?

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 12\. Trigger events increase qualification

**Classification:** Buying signal **Source:** [K] + [G]

**Explanation**

Changes in the customer's environment can turn latent pain into immediate demand.

**Example**

A contractor wins three large projects and suddenly needs to coordinate twice as many subcontractors.

**Observable evidence**

Recent or upcoming change.

**AI inference**

Trigger events should increase opportunity priority.

**Next action**

Determine timeline and consequences.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 13\. Authority matters

**Classification:** Qualification rule **Source:** [K]

**Explanation**

A person loving the product does not necessarily create a sale.

Understand who can:

* approve;
* reject;
* allocate budget;
* approve IT access;
* authorize deployment.

**Example**

A Bauleiter desperately wants Feldleiter.

The Geschäftsführer controls software purchases.

**Observable evidence**

Named decision-makers and decision process.

**AI inference**

Distinguish:

`user`

`champion`

`influencer`

`economic buyer`

where relevant.

**Next action**

Get the relevant stakeholder involved.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 14\. A champion is valuable

**Classification:** Buying signal **Source:** [K] + [G]

**Explanation**

Someone inside the organization actively helping the sale progress is materially different from someone who merely likes the product.

**Observable evidence**

They:

* introduce colleagues;
* provide internal information;
* explain approval processes;
* advocate internally;
* arrange meetings;
* help gather data.

**AI inference**

Champion behavior is stronger evidence than verbal enthusiasm.

**Next action**

Equip the champion to advance the opportunity internally.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 15\. Curiosity is not qualification

**Classification:** Disqualification signal **Source:** [K]

**Explanation**

Founders receive many conversations from people interested in technology but unlikely to buy.

Particularly with AI, curiosity can produce enormous false-positive demand.

**Example**

“Wow, AI agents for construction sound fascinating.”

but:

* no operational problem;
* no budget;
* no project;
* no urgency.

**Observable evidence**

Questions remain technology-oriented rather than business-oriented.

**AI inference**

Classify as:

**interesting conversation — unqualified opportunity**.

**Next action**

Return to problem evidence or stop pursuing commercially.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

# V. Discovery

## 16\. Discovery precedes presentation

**Classification:** Discovery rule **Source:** [K]

**Explanation**

Do not immediately launch into product features.

Understand the customer's situation first.

**Example**

Before showing an automated supplier-agent system, understand:

* tender frequency;
* who identifies suppliers;
* who emails them;
* response rates;
* how prices are compared;
* what errors happen.

**Observable evidence**

Substantial customer-specific information precedes solution presentation.

**AI inference**

Premature solution explanation risks both poor selling and contaminated discovery.

**Next action**

Continue diagnosis until you understand whether the product actually maps to the problem.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 17\. Discover workflow, not merely complaints

**Classification:** Discovery rule **Source:** [K] + [E]

**Explanation**

Pain is easier to evaluate when embedded in the customer's actual process.

**Example**

Map:

`LV arrives → estimator identifies materials → suppliers selected → RFQs written → replies arrive → spreadsheet comparison → offer created`.

**Observable evidence**

Actors, steps, systems and handoffs become explicit.

**AI inference**

The system can locate where the proposed product creates value.

**Next action**

Probe bottlenecks, costs and failure points.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 18\. Understand desired outcomes

**Classification:** Discovery rule **Source:** [K]

**Explanation**

Customers purchase outcomes rather than features.

**Example**

Desired outcome might not be:

AI sends supplier emails.

It might be:

Tender preparation drops from two days to four hours without increasing pricing risk.

**Observable evidence**

Customer describes improved operational state.

**AI inference**

Value proposition should anchor on outcome.

**Next action**

Confirm what success would look like.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

# VI. Buying signals and commitment

The harness should strongly weight **customer actions over customer language**.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 19\. Asking about price is a buying signal

**Classification:** Buying signal **Source:** [K] + [G]

**Explanation**

Price questions often indicate movement from conceptual interest toward evaluation.

But they are not proof of purchase.

**Observable evidence**

“What would this cost?”

**AI inference**

Increase commercial-interest confidence modestly.

**Next action**

Discuss pricing only after sufficient context exists.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 20\. Asking implementation questions is stronger than praise

**Classification:** Buying signal **Source:** [G], strongly aligned with [K]

Examples:

* How long does setup take?
* Does it integrate with DATEV?
* Can this run on our server?
* Can three Bauleiter use it?
* How do permissions work?

**AI inference**

Customer is mentally simulating adoption.

**Next action**

Investigate implementation requirements and decision process.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 21\. Introducing other stakeholders is strong evidence

**Classification:** Commitment signal **Source:** [K]

**Explanation**

A prospect spending political or social capital by involving others is demonstrating meaningful engagement.

**Observable evidence**

“You should show this to our Geschäftsführer.”

**AI inference**

This is materially stronger than:

“Looks great.”

**Next action**

Schedule the stakeholder conversation immediately.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 22\. Sharing proprietary information is a commitment signal

**Classification:** Commitment signal **Source:** [K] + [E]

Examples:

* sample spreadsheets;
* workflow screenshots;
* historical tenders;
* anonymized invoices;
* process documentation.

**AI inference**

Customer is investing effort and trust.

**Next action**

Use the artifacts to validate workflow and pilot feasibility.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 23\. Concrete calendar movement is evidence

**Classification:** Commitment signal **Source:** [K]

Weak:

“Let's stay in touch.”

Strong:

“Let's meet Thursday at 10 with our Kalkulator.”

**AI inference**

Only the latter represents meaningful advancement.

**Next action**

Create a dated next step with participants and objective.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

# VII. Progressing the sale

## 24\. Every live opportunity needs a next step

**Classification:** Sales process rule **Source:** [K]

This is one of the most useful rules for your harness.

A sales call should not end with:

“I'll send something over.”

It should end with a mutually understood next event.

**Example**

Thursday, 10:00: review a real supplier request with Geschäftsführer and Kalkulator.

**Observable evidence**

There is:

* an action;
* owner;
* date;
* purpose.

**AI inference**

Opportunity without a concrete next step should lose pipeline confidence.

**Next action**

Secure the next commitment before ending the interaction where possible.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 25\. Advancement matters more than friendliness

**Classification:** Qualification rule **Source:** [K]

Founders are easily fooled by friendly prospects.

A successful meeting is not:

“They loved the demo.”

A successful meeting advances the buying process.

**Observable evidence**

Advancement includes:

* technical evaluation;
* another stakeholder;
* data access;
* pilot;
* procurement step;
* pricing discussion.

**AI inference**

Record **stage movement**, not meeting sentiment.

**Next action**

Determine what must happen next to advance.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

# VIII. Transitioning from research into sales

This is particularly important for your harness.

Kazanjy's methodology supports founder selling, but the following explicit transition protocol is primarily **[E]**, synthesized with the discovery books.

## 26\. Do not silently change modes

**Classification:** Anti-pattern **Source:** [E]

Suppose the founder says:

“I'm researching how construction companies manage procurement.”

The participant believes they are helping research.

Halfway through, the founder starts pitching software.

That contaminates both:

* the relationship;
* the evidence.

The interviewee starts behaving like a sales prospect.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 27\. Finish the evidence-bearing part first

**Classification:** Discovery rule **Source:** [E]

Before revealing a solution, capture:

* current workflow;
* problem;
* examples;
* frequency;
* consequences;
* current solution;
* current spend;
* priority;
* recent attempts to fix it.

Only then should a possible commercial transition occur.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 28\. Require evidence before transitioning

**Classification:** Qualification rule **Source:** [E]

The harness should not transition simply because the founder has something to sell.

A reasonable threshold is evidence of:

**problem + relevance + materiality**

with at least some evidence of urgency or active search.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 29\. Explicitly ask permission to change modes

**Classification:** Sales process rule **Source:** [E]

A clean transition might be:

“You've been helping me understand how you handle this today. Based on what you've described, something I'm working on might actually be relevant. Would it be useful if I explained it?”

If they say no, stay in research mode.

This preserves epistemic integrity.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 30\. Freeze pre-pitch evidence

**Classification:** Synthesis / sales process rule **Source:** [E]

Once the solution has been revealed, later statements should be tagged separately.

For example:

**PRE\_SOLUTION\_EVIDENCE**

“Supplier follow-up takes me about six hours per tender.”

versus:

**POST\_SOLUTION\_REACTION**

“Yes, I'd definitely use your automated version.”

The first is strong discovery evidence.

The second may be heavily contaminated by your pitch.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

# IX. Pilot discussions

## 31\. A pilot should test a commercial hypothesis

**Classification:** Sales process rule **Source:** [K] + [G]

A pilot is not:

“Let's play around with the product.”

It should answer whether the solution can create sufficient customer value.

**Example**

For supplier procurement:

Run three real tenders through the system during four weeks.

**Observable evidence**

Real workflow and real business consequences are involved.

**AI inference**

Pilot quality rises when customer exposure to reality increases.

**Next action**

Define scope and success criteria.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 32\. Define pilot success before starting

**Classification:** Sales process rule **Source:** [G], compatible with [K]

Example criteria:

* reduce RFQ preparation time by 70%;
* contact ≥90% of selected suppliers automatically;
* capture replies correctly;
* reduce manual comparison work.

**AI inference**

Without pre-agreed criteria, a pilot can succeed technically while producing no sale.

**Next action**

Agree what result would justify continuing commercially.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 33\. Customer contribution matters

**Classification:** Commitment signal **Source:** [K] + [E]

A pilot requiring nothing from the customer produces weak evidence.

Prefer mutual commitment.

Customer may provide:

* staff time;
* data;
* access;
* implementation effort;
* money.

**AI inference**

The more genuine sacrifice the customer makes, the stronger the commercial evidence.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

# X. Early pricing

## 34\. Asking for money creates information

**Classification:** Founder-specific heuristic **Source:** [K]

Early founders often postpone pricing because they do not know the “correct” price.

But avoiding the question prevents learning.

**Example**

Ten companies saying:

“I'd use this.”

provides less pricing evidence than one company saying:

“I'll pay €500/month.”

**Observable evidence**

Actual willingness to transact.

**AI inference**

Treat stated willingness-to-pay as weaker than real purchasing behavior.

**Next action**

Introduce a concrete commercial proposal once sufficient value has been established.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 35\. Early pricing can be imperfect

**Classification:** Founder-specific heuristic **Source:** [K]

The initial objective is not perfect price optimization.

It is discovering:

* whether customers will pay;
* approximate value;
* what pricing objections appear;
* which unit makes commercial sense.

**Example**

Initial automation engagement:

€1,000 setup + €300/month.

Later customers may reveal that another structure works better.

**AI inference**

Do not delay selling while attempting theoretical pricing precision.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 36\. Free is dangerous evidence

**Classification:** Anti-pattern **Source:** [K] + [G]

Free products and pilots remove one of the most important commercial tests.

A free user demonstrates:

willingness to try.

not necessarily:

willingness to buy.

**Appropriate next action**

Where possible, require some meaningful commercial or operational commitment.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

# XI. Objections

## 37\. Objections contain diagnostic information

**Classification:** Discovery rule **Source:** [K]

Do not treat objections merely as barriers to defeat.

They reveal constraints.

**Example**

“We can't give an AI system access to supplier email.”

Possible underlying problems:

* security;
* organizational trust;
* IT policy;
* perceived risk.

**AI inference**

Record objection category and underlying reason.

**Next action**

Understand the concern before responding.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 38\. Repeated objections should modify the product or sales system

**Classification:** Founder-specific heuristic **Source:** [K]

If the same objection appears repeatedly, it may represent:

* product deficiency;
* ICP mismatch;
* positioning issue;
* missing integration;
* missing trust mechanism.

**Example**

Five contractors say:

“We need this inside Microsoft 365 rather than another standalone inbox.”

That is product-market information.

**Next action**

Feed recurring objections back into product and ICP decisions.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 39\. “Too expensive” is not sufficiently diagnostic

**Classification:** Anti-pattern **Source:** [K] + [G]

It can mean:

* genuinely too expensive;
* insufficient pain;
* unclear value;
* wrong buyer;
* no budget now;
* low trust;
* weak urgency.

**AI inference**

Never automatically translate:

`price objection → reduce price`.

**Next action**

Determine the actual reason.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

# XII. Follow-up

## 40\. Follow-up is part of selling

**Classification:** Follow-up rule **Source:** [K]

B2B transactions frequently require repeated interactions.

A lack of immediate response does not necessarily mean rejection.

**Observable evidence**

Opportunity remains open but inactive.

**AI inference**

Track:

* last interaction;
* promised action;
* next follow-up date;
* blocker.

**Next action**

Follow up systematically rather than from memory.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 41\. Follow-up should add context rather than merely nag

**Classification:** Follow-up rule **Source:** [K] + [G]

Weak:

Just checking in.

Better:

You mentioned the new project begins October 15\. You also wanted Thomas involved before then. Should we review the pilot scope next week?

**AI inference**

Good follow-up references agreed context and progression.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

# XIII. Pipeline discipline

## 42\. Use a CRM earlier than feels necessary

**Classification:** Sales process rule **Source:** [K]

Even founder-led selling becomes impossible to reason about if opportunities live only in memory.

Track at least:

* account;
* contact;
* source;
* stage;
* pain;
* potential value;
* last interaction;
* next action;
* expected timing;
* outcome.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 43\. Pipeline stages need exit criteria

**Classification:** Sales process rule **Source:** [K] + [G]

Weak CRM stages:

Interested Very interested Hot

Better:

Contacted Discovery completed Qualified Solution presented Pilot agreed Commercial proposal Closed won/lost

**AI inference**

Stage should represent observable customer behavior, not salesperson optimism.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 44\. Do not let dead opportunities pollute the pipeline

**Classification:** Anti-pattern **Source:** [K]

An opportunity that has no:

* urgency;
* next action;
* responsive stakeholder;
* commercial path

should eventually be closed or recycled.

**AI inference**

“Maybe someday” is not an active opportunity.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 45\. Measure conversion, not just activity

**Classification:** Sales process rule **Source:** [K]

Track funnel behavior such as:

`prospects → responses → meetings → qualified → proposals → wins`.

This helps diagnose bottlenecks.

**Example**

If:

* many meetings happen;
* almost nobody qualifies;

then targeting or problem hypothesis may be wrong.

If:

* many qualify;
* few buy;

then solution, pricing, trust or buying process may be wrong.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

# XIV. Learning from lost deals

## 46\. A lost deal is product information

**Classification:** Founder-specific heuristic **Source:** [K]

Early founders should investigate why qualified opportunities do not close.

Potential reasons:

* insufficient pain;
* wrong timing;
* no budget;
* missing feature;
* implementation difficulty;
* incumbent advantage;
* organizational politics;
* lack of trust.

**AI inference**

Loss reasons should feed:

* ICP;
* messaging;
* roadmap;
* pricing;
* qualification.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 47\. Distinguish loss from disqualification

**Classification:** Sales process rule **Source:** [E]

These should not be mixed.

**Disqualified**

The prospect was never a good opportunity.

Example:

Two-person company with no relevant workflow.

**Lost**

The opportunity was real but another outcome occurred.

Example:

Qualified company chose an incumbent solution.

This distinction matters enormously when evaluating product-market evidence.

# XV. Turning founder sales into repeatable knowledge

## 48\. Founder sales is a learning loop

**Classification:** Principle **Source:** [K]

The founder should progressively learn:

`who buys → why they buy → what triggers buying → who decides → what objections occur → what messaging works → how long closing takes`.

That knowledge eventually becomes the company's sales playbook.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 49\. Look for repeated patterns before scaling

**Classification:** Founder-specific heuristic **Source:** [K]

Do not hire salespeople merely because the founder dislikes selling.

First establish some repeatability.

The founder should understand:

* target customer;
* pain;
* pitch;
* qualification;
* objections;
* sales cycle;
* pricing;
* closing motion.

Only then can another salesperson reasonably reproduce it.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 50\. Founder sales knowledge should become explicit

**Classification:** Sales process rule **Source:** [K]

Capture:

* winning ICP attributes;
* common pain statements;
* discovery questions;
* disqualification criteria;
* objection patterns;
* pricing patterns;
* stakeholder maps;
* typical sales cycle;
* successful case studies.

For your harness, this is extremely important because the system can continuously turn interviews into structured sales intelligence.

# Research → Commercial Transition State Machine

For your system, I would implement approximately this logic:

```
RESEARCH
    ↓
Evidence of recurring problem?
    ├── no → continue research
    └── yes
          ↓
PROBLEM DISCOVERY
          ↓
Problem significant + relevant to founder's possible solution?
    ├── no → record evidence, remain research-only
    └── yes
          ↓
Does participant show urgency / active effort / desire to solve?
    ├── no → record as problem without current buying intent
    └── yes
          ↓
Ask permission to switch to commercial conversation
    ├── no → end research cleanly
    └── yes
          ↓
SALES DISCOVERY
          ↓
Evaluate fit + pain + urgency + authority + purchase path
          ↓
QUALIFICATION
    ├── disqualified → close/recycle
    └── qualified
          ↓
SOLUTION / COMMERCIAL DISCUSSION
          ↓
Concrete next commitment?
    ├── no → follow-up or close
    └── yes
          ↓
PILOT / PROPOSAL
          ↓
Actual commercial commitment

```

The important part is that **everything learned before the mode switch remains uncontaminated discovery evidence**, while everything afterwards is tagged as sales interaction evidence.

# Specific contamination rules

These are **[E]**, derived from combining Kazanjy with the earlier discovery methodologies.

The AI interviewer should enforce:

1. **No product explanation before problem evidence is sufficiently captured.**
2. **No “Would you use X?” questions during research.**
3. **No pricing questions framed hypothetically before current cost/value is understood.**
4. **Do not reinterpret post-demo enthusiasm as pre-existing pain.**
5. **Record the exact moment a solution is revealed.**
6. **Tag subsequent evidence as POST\_PITCH.**
7. **Require explicit permission before changing from research to sales mode.**
8. **Never force a commercial transition merely because the participant fits the ICP.**

This solves a major problem in mixed founder interviews: preserving the quality of research while still allowing the founder to sell when genuine demand emerges.

# Kazanjy vs generic B2B practice vs harness extrapolation

A useful boundary is:

**Strongly Kazanjy / Founding Sales**

* founder must learn to sell;
* founder-led outbound;
* explicit ICP;
* segmentation;
* targeted prospecting;
* qualification;
* discovery before presentation;
* understanding customer pain;
* navigating stakeholders;
* gaining commitments;
* concrete next steps;
* commercial advancement;
* CRM/pipeline discipline;
* measuring the funnel;
* objection learning;
* repeated follow-up;
* learning from wins/losses;
* developing repeatable sales motion before handing sales off.

**Generic B2B concepts compatible with Kazanjy**

* formal champion/economic-buyer terminology;
* quantified ROI frameworks;
* formal pilot success scorecards;
* detailed procurement mapping;
* security review qualification;
* MEDDIC/MEDDPICC-style qualification;
* sophisticated mutual action plans.

Do not falsely attribute those frameworks specifically to Kazanjy.

**Harness-specific extrapolation**

* explicit research/problem-discovery/sales-discovery modes;
* mode-switch permission;
* PRE\_PITCH versus POST\_PITCH evidence;
* automated commercial-intent scoring;
* evidence hierarchy;
* AI-driven transition decisions;
* cross-interview pattern extraction.

Those are architectural consequences of integrating *Founding Sales* with the other methodologies rather than teachings to attribute directly to the book.

# Canonical Founder-Led Sales Rules for the Harness

1. **Founder-led sales is part of discovery until a repeatable sales motion exists.**
2. **Define an ICP narrowly enough that inclusion and exclusion decisions are possible.**
3. **Keep account fit separate from contact/persona fit.**
4. **Segment customers when pains, buyers or buying processes materially differ.**
5. **Build explicit prospect lists rather than relying on random conversations.**
6. **Use targeted founder outreach before trying to scale acquisition.**
7. **Do not confuse a willingness to talk with a willingness to buy.**
8. **Do not confuse curiosity about technology with business pain.**
9. **Qualify pain using actual consequences, frequency and existing effort.**
10. **Investigate the current solution because doing nothing and manual processes are competitors too.**
11. **Pain without urgency is not necessarily a current opportunity.**
12. **Identify triggering events that make solving the problem timely.**
13. **Determine who uses, champions, influences, approves and pays for the solution.**
14. **Discovery must precede presentation.**
15. **Understand the customer's actual workflow before mapping your solution onto it.**
16. **Anchor commercial value in business outcomes rather than features.**
17. **Do not switch from research to selling until useful pre-pitch evidence has been captured.**
18. **Explicitly obtain permission before moving from research into a commercial conversation.**
19. **Tag all evidence before and after solution exposure separately.**
20. **Weight actions more strongly than statements.**
21. **A stakeholder introduction is stronger than praise.**
22. **Sharing internal data is stronger than stated interest.**
23. **Allocating staff time is stronger than curiosity.**
24. **Agreeing to a pilot is stronger than requesting a demo.**
25. **Paying is stronger than every preceding signal.**
26. **Every active opportunity should have a concrete next action.**
27. **A next action should ideally have an owner, date and objective.**
28. **Measure advancement rather than meeting sentiment.**
29. **Use pilots to test real value, not merely product functionality.**
30. **Define pilot success before the pilot begins.**
31. **Prefer pilots involving meaningful customer commitment.**
32. **Introduce real pricing early enough to test commercial reality.**
33. **Do not mistake free usage for willingness to pay.**
34. **Treat objections as diagnostic evidence before trying to overcome them.**
35. **Repeated objections should influence ICP, product, positioning or process.**
36. **Do not automatically interpret price objections as evidence that the numerical price is wrong.**
37. **Follow up systematically rather than relying on founder memory.**
38. **Follow-up should reference context and advance the process.**
39. **Track all real opportunities in a CRM or equivalent structured system.**
40. **Pipeline stages must correspond to observable customer actions.**
41. **Do not keep dead opportunities indefinitely classified as active.**
42. **Measure conversion between stages to locate failures in the sales system.**
43. **Distinguish disqualified prospects from legitimate lost deals.**
44. **Analyze lost deals for ICP, product, messaging, pricing and process lessons.**
45. **Continuously accumulate evidence about who buys, why they buy and what makes them buy now.**
46. **Do not delegate founder sales until enough of that knowledge is explicit and repeatable.**
47. **The goal of early founder selling is not merely revenue; it is a repeatable model connecting customer characteristics, pain, buying behavior and successful commercial outcomes.**

For your combined harness, the most important conceptual handoff is therefore:

**Mom Test / Portigal / Hansen / Torres / JTBD determine whether a problem exists. Testing Business Ideas determines what evidence is still missing. Founding Sales determines whether that problem can become a real B2B transaction and how to systematically advance it toward one.**
