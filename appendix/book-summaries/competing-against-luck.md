# 10\. Competing Against Luck — Clayton Christensen et al.

**Weight: STRONG — conceptual JTBD extraction**

The main value of *Competing Against Luck* for the harness is not interview mechanics. It is a **causal lens for interpreting what customers are actually trying to accomplish**. Kalbach is much better for turning JTBD into structured maps, statements, and research artifacts. Christensen is stronger on the mental model that prevents you from defining the market incorrectly in the first place.

I’ll mark concepts as:

* **[Christensen — high confidence]**: clearly central to *Competing Against Luck*.
* **[Christensen/JTBD — moderate confidence]**: consistent with the book, but terminology or formulation is also common in broader JTBD literature.
* **[Broader JTBD interpretation]**: useful extension, but should not be represented in the harness as uniquely Christensen.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## 1\. A Job Is the Progress Someone Is Trying to Make

**[Christensen — high confidence]**

### Explanation

A job is not a task in the narrow sense and not a product requirement.

It is the **progress a person is trying to make in a particular circumstance**.

The customer does not fundamentally want a product. They "hire" something because they are trying to move from a current situation toward a preferred situation.

This shifts the discovery unit from:

"What feature does the customer want?"

to:

"What are they trying to make happen, and why does that matter now?"

The word **progress** is important. A job contains movement: something about the current situation is unsatisfactory or incomplete, and the person wants a different state.

### Example

A construction-company owner does not necessarily have the job:

"Manage employees with scheduling software."

Their actual progress might be:

"Get tomorrow's crews assigned to the right sites without spending my evening calling everyone."

Scheduling software is merely one possible solution.

### Implication for interviewing

Ask about the movement between states:

* What were they trying to accomplish?
* What triggered the need?
* What made the previous situation unacceptable?
* What outcome were they hoping to reach?
* What made the issue important at that particular moment?

The interviewer should resist converting the story into a software requirement too quickly.

### Implication for opportunity discovery

Opportunities should initially be represented as **desired progress**, not products or features.

Weak:

Automated scheduling app.

Better:

Reduce the effort and uncertainty involved in assigning tomorrow's crews when plans change late in the day.

This creates a wider solution space.

# 2\. Jobs Exist in Circumstances

**[Christensen — high confidence]**

### Explanation

Jobs cannot be understood independently of the circumstances in which they arise.

The same person may "hire" completely different solutions depending on:

* location;
* time;
* urgency;
* available resources;
* who else is involved;
* what happened immediately before;
* organizational constraints;
* emotional state;
* risk.

Christensen's argument is partly a criticism of segmentation based on customer attributes.

Knowing **who the customer is** is often less predictive than knowing **what circumstance they are in when the need emerges**.

### Example

A contractor may normally coordinate workers through WhatsApp.

That solution becomes inadequate when:

* three sites change simultaneously;
* a worker calls in sick;
* subcontractors must be reassigned;
* the owner is driving;
* clients need immediate confirmation.

The opportunity may therefore be associated with **high-change coordination circumstances**, not simply "construction businesses with 10–30 workers."

### Implication for interviewing

Always reconstruct context around important behavior.

Useful probes:

* What was happening when this became a problem?
* Who was involved?
* What had changed?
* What made this instance difficult?
* Was this a normal day or an unusual situation?
* What constraints did you have?

An AI interviewer should treat contextless statements such as:

"Scheduling is difficult."

as incomplete evidence.

### Implication for opportunity discovery

Segmenting opportunities by **circumstance** can be more powerful than segmenting solely by company size or demographic characteristics.

For example:

Companies managing workers across multiple changing job sites

may reveal a stronger job than:

Construction companies with 10–50 employees.

# 3\. Customers "Hire" Solutions

**[Christensen — high confidence]**

### Explanation

Products and services are metaphorically **hired to perform a job**.

This is more than colorful language.

It forces the researcher to ask:

Why did this solution get selected in this circumstance?

rather than merely:

Why does the customer like this product?

Products have roles in customers' lives or workflows.

When circumstances change, a previously successful solution can be fired and another solution hired.

### Example

A small company might hire:

* WhatsApp for coordination;
* Excel for scheduling;
* paper printouts for workers;
* the owner's memory;
* morning phone calls.

None of these needs to be a purpose-built scheduling system.

They are still solutions being "hired."

### Implication for interviewing

When the customer describes an existing tool, probe its employment:

* Why did you start using it?
* What were you doing before?
* What makes you keep using it?
* When does it work well?
* When does it fail?
* What would make you replace it?

Do not assume that an existing SaaS product is the relevant incumbent.

### Implication for opportunity discovery

The true competitive set should be derived from **solutions performing the same job**, not products belonging to the same category.

Your future SaaS may compete more directly against:

WhatsApp + Excel + the owner's habitual workflow

than against another SaaS vendor.

# 4\. Customers Also "Fire" Solutions

**[Christensen — high confidence]**

### Explanation

Switching contains particularly valuable evidence because customers have already demonstrated that something became insufficient.

When someone abandons one solution and adopts another, there is usually a sequence of causes:

* dissatisfaction accumulated;
* circumstances changed;
* another alternative became available;
* inertia was overcome;
* perceived switching risk became acceptable.

Studying the firing decision can reveal the job more clearly than asking customers what features they value.

### Example

A contractor stops managing everything in a WhatsApp group and creates a shared spreadsheet.

The spreadsheet itself is less interesting than:

What happened that made WhatsApp no longer good enough?

Perhaps:

* tasks were forgotten;
* information became impossible to find;
* too many workers were involved;
* accountability became unclear.

Those events expose the underlying job.

### Implication for interviewing

Switching stories deserve aggressive follow-up.

Ask for the timeline:

What were you using before?

What first made you consider changing it?

Was there a particular incident?

Why didn't you change earlier?

What finally caused you to act?

This turns a general preference into observable causal evidence.

### Implication for opportunity discovery

Repeated switching triggers are excellent indicators of opportunity.

If multiple businesses independently abandon similar workarounds under similar circumstances, you may have identified a structural breakdown point.

# 5\. Real Competition Is Defined by the Job

**[Christensen — high confidence]**

### Explanation

Traditional competitive analysis starts with product categories.

JTBD reverses this:

Two solutions compete when customers consider them plausible ways of making the same progress.

Therefore competitors can look completely different.

A SaaS application can compete with:

* spreadsheets;
* outsourcing;
* manual labor;
* doing nothing;
* postponement;
* an employee;
* a phone call.

### Example

Suppose the job is:

Make sure workers know where they need to be tomorrow.

Possible competitors include:

* workforce-management software;
* WhatsApp;
* phone calls;
* printed schedules;
* a foreman;
* morning meetings.

Analyzing only "workforce management SaaS competitors" would miss most of the current market.

### Implication for interviewing

Ask:

What do you currently do when this happens?

not:

Which software do you use for this?

Also ask:

What other ways have you tried?

and:

What happens if you simply don't solve it?

### Implication for opportunity discovery

Competitor maps should emerge **after understanding the job**.

The harness should maintain:

**Job → currently hired alternatives**

rather than:

**Product idea → similar products.**

# 6\. Causality Matters More Than Correlation

**[Christensen — high confidence]**

### Explanation

This is one of the most important conceptual claims in the book.

Companies frequently discover correlations such as:

Customers of type X buy product Y.

But correlation does not explain **why** the purchase happens.

JTBD aims to uncover the causal mechanism:

Under circumstance C, customers seek progress P, which causes them to hire solution S.

Demographic or behavioral datasets may predict outcomes somewhat, but they often provide weak guidance about innovation because they do not explain the underlying reason.

### Example

You may observe:

Construction companies with more than 20 workers are more likely to buy workforce software.

That correlation alone says little.

Perhaps software adoption is actually triggered when:

crew coordination becomes decentralized across multiple simultaneous sites.

A 12-person company operating five sites might therefore experience the job more intensely than a 40-person company working on one location.

### Implication for interviewing

The interviewer should continually search for causal sequences:

**circumstance → struggle → action → choice → outcome**

rather than simply collecting:

**attribute → opinion.**

Statements such as:

"Companies like ours need better software"

are weak.

Stories explaining:

"Once we opened our third simultaneous site, I could no longer keep everything in my head..."

are far more valuable.

### Implication for opportunity discovery

Do not promote correlations directly into opportunities.

Instead ask:

What mechanism explains why this problem becomes important?

Opportunity confidence should rise when similar causal patterns occur across interviews.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

# 7\. Demographics Do Not Define Jobs

**[Christensen — high confidence]**

### Explanation

Age, company size, industry, income, geography, job title, and similar characteristics can be useful descriptors.

But they do not necessarily explain why customers make particular choices.

People with very different demographic attributes can have the same job.

People with identical demographic attributes can have completely different jobs.

### Example

Two 20-person construction companies might look identical in CRM segmentation.

Company A:

* stable projects;
* workers stay at one site for months;
* little coordination overhead.

Company B:

* five concurrent projects;
* frequent site changes;
* temporary subcontractors;
* daily reassignment.

The second company may have an acute coordination job that the first does not.

### Implication for interviewing

Use demographics primarily to contextualize the story.

Do not assume:

"They are a construction company, therefore they have job X."

Discover the job through actual behavior and circumstances.

### Implication for opportunity discovery

ICP definition can eventually combine:

**actor + circumstance + job intensity**

rather than relying mainly on firmographics.

For example:

Owner-managed trade businesses coordinating mobile teams across multiple simultaneous locations with frequent schedule changes.

That is considerably more actionable than:

SMEs in construction.

# 8\. Product Categories Do Not Define Jobs

**[Christensen — high confidence]**

### Explanation

A product category is an artifact of the supplier side of the market.

Customers usually begin from their situation, not from a category taxonomy.

A customer experiencing a problem does not necessarily think:

"I need a field-service management platform."

They think:

"I need everybody to know where they're going tomorrow."

This distinction matters because category framing dramatically constrains solution discovery.

### Example

If you start research around:

construction scheduling software

you will mostly discover scheduling features.

If you investigate:

how construction managers ensure tomorrow's work happens despite changing conditions,

you may discover problems involving:

* staffing;
* material availability;
* instructions;
* travel;
* approvals;
* client communication.

### Implication for interviewing

Avoid category-language unless the participant introduces it.

Prefer workflow language:

How do you currently handle this?

over:

What scheduling software do you use?

### Implication for opportunity discovery

Early opportunity representations should remain category-independent.

Do not let an existing product idea become the frame through which evidence is interpreted.

# 9\. Functional, Emotional, and Social Dimensions

**[Christensen/JTBD — moderate-to-high confidence]**

### Explanation

Jobs are not purely functional.

The desired progress can include:

**Functional**

Get tomorrow's schedule organized.

**Emotional**

Stop feeling anxious that something has been forgotten.

**Social**

Appear organized and reliable to workers or clients.

These dimensions often coexist.

A purely functional interpretation may miss why customers strongly prefer one solution over another.

### Example

A construction manager may want software partly because it coordinates workers.

But the deeper desired progress might also include:

* not feeling permanently reactive;
* avoiding embarrassment when clients expose coordination mistakes;
* appearing professional as the company grows.

### Implication for interviewing

When strong emotional language appears, investigate it rather than dismissing it as irrelevant.

Useful probes:

Why was that frustrating?

What happens when that goes wrong?

Who notices?

How does that affect you?

The interviewer should not artificially force every job into three dimensions, however.

### Implication for opportunity discovery

Emotional and social consequences can help explain **job intensity** and willingness to adopt a solution.

Two operational inconveniences of equal time cost may have radically different commercial value if one exposes the owner to client embarrassment, financial risk, or loss of control.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

# 10\. A Job Often Has Multiple Dimensions Simultaneously

**[Christensen/JTBD — moderate confidence]**

### Explanation

It is usually misleading to separate functional, emotional, and social jobs too rigidly.

A single hiring decision may satisfy several dimensions.

For example:

"Keep project information organized"

can simultaneously mean:

* avoid errors;
* feel in control;
* appear competent.

The dimensions help explain the decision; they should not necessarily become separate opportunity records.

### Example

An owner hiring accounting software may want to:

* generate accurate invoices;
* stop worrying about missing paperwork;
* appear professional to their accountant.

### Implication for interviewing

Follow the participant's causal chain instead of running a checklist:

functional question → emotional question → social question.

Let dimensions emerge naturally.

### Implication for opportunity discovery

Use these dimensions as annotations around a job.

Do not automatically generate three jobs from one customer story.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

# 11\. The Switch Is a Rich Source of Causal Evidence

**[Christensen — high confidence]**

### Explanation

Customer choice becomes particularly understandable when reconstructed as a transition:

**old behavior → dissatisfaction/change → search → decision → new behavior**

Switching exposes both:

* what pushed the customer away from the old state;
* what attracted them toward the new state.

This idea became even more explicit in broader JTBD "switch interview" practice.

### Example

A business starts using dedicated time-tracking software.

The useful discovery question isn't:

"What do you like about time tracking?"

The useful story is:

What happened in the weeks before you installed it?

Perhaps payroll errors, disputed hours, or a client billing dispute created the trigger.

### Implication for interviewing

Treat adoption moments as forensic events.

Reconstruct them chronologically.

Do not settle for retrospective explanations such as:

"We needed more efficiency."

Ask what actually happened.

### Implication for opportunity discovery

Trigger events are powerful opportunity boundaries.

A business may tolerate an inefficient process for years until some transition occurs:

* hiring;
* regulatory pressure;
* multiple locations;
* new management;
* customer complaints;
* volume growth.

Those transitions can be excellent target conditions.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

# 12\. The Job Is Stable Even When Solutions Change

**[Christensen — high confidence]**

### Explanation

Solutions evolve rapidly.

Underlying jobs can remain comparatively stable.

People wanted:

communicate across distance

long before email, messaging apps, or video calls.

This means innovation should anchor itself in customer progress rather than today's technology.

### Example

Construction managers have always needed:

make sure workers understand what work happens next.

That job existed before:

* mobile apps;
* WhatsApp;
* spreadsheets;
* cloud systems.

### Implication for interviewing

Ask about how the work was accomplished historically.

What did you do before this tool?

This can reveal which parts of the job are persistent versus artifacts of current software.

### Implication for opportunity discovery

Stable jobs are often stronger foundations for products than transient technical preferences.

Avoid opportunities such as:

AI chatbot for scheduling

until you understand the durable job it would serve.

# 13\. Innovation Can Be Built Around a Job Rather Than a Product

**[Christensen — high confidence]**

### Explanation

The book argues that companies can organize innovation around a deep understanding of the job rather than around product improvement alone.

Once the job is understood, the company can optimize the complete experience of helping the customer make progress.

This may require changing more than the product itself:

* onboarding;
* pricing;
* delivery;
* support;
* workflow;
* integrations;
* business model.

### Example

If the job is:

Make tomorrow's field operation reliable despite last-minute changes,

the solution might require more than a scheduling screen.

It may involve:

* worker confirmations;
* notifications;
* absence handling;
* replacement suggestions;
* instruction delivery;
* escalation.

### Implication for interviewing

Probe the entire journey surrounding the job rather than one interaction with the proposed product.

### Implication for opportunity discovery

A strong opportunity may span multiple conventional product categories.

The harness should avoid prematurely fragmenting evidence according to anticipated product modules.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

# 14\. Jobs Explain Why Customers Choose, Not Merely What They Prefer

**[Christensen — high confidence]**

### Explanation

Preference questions often produce weak innovation evidence:

"Would you prefer X or Y?"

JTBD tries to explain actual choice.

The core question is:

What caused this solution to become the customer's preferred response to this situation?

Observed adoption, abandonment, payment, workaround creation, and switching therefore carry greater explanatory value than abstract preference.

### Example

Someone saying:

"A centralized dashboard would be nice"

does not reveal a job particularly well.

Someone spending Sunday evening building an Excel workbook after their third coordination failure provides much stronger evidence.

### Implication for interviewing

Prioritize:

* choices;
* changes;
* purchases;
* workarounds;
* switching;
* repeated behavior.

### Implication for opportunity discovery

Preference alone should generate hypotheses.

Behavior demonstrating attempted progress should generate stronger opportunity evidence.

This aligns closely with *The Mom Test* and should not be implemented separately as redundant logic.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

# 15\. Existing Workarounds Reveal the Job

**[Broader JTBD interpretation, strongly compatible with Christensen]**

### Explanation

When customers invent ad hoc methods, they reveal that the underlying job exists even though no ideal solution does.

The workaround demonstrates that making progress matters enough to spend effort.

### Example

A business coordinates workers with:

* WhatsApp;
* Excel;
* handwritten notes;
* phone calls;
* photos.

The messy stack itself may reveal an unmet job.

### Implication for interviewing

Ask how the workaround developed.

Especially investigate:

* manual steps;
* duplicated information;
* exceptions;
* maintenance burden.

### Implication for opportunity discovery

Workaround complexity can be useful evidence of unmet need.

But do not automatically assume:

ugly workflow = SaaS opportunity.

A workaround can be ugly yet perfectly acceptable because the problem is rare or low-value.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

# 16\. "Why Now?" Is Often More Important Than "Why?"

**[Broader JTBD / switch-interview interpretation]**

### Explanation

People frequently live with imperfect solutions for years.

The critical causal question is therefore not only:

Why did they want something better?

but:

Why did they act at this particular moment?

The triggering circumstance reveals the point at which latent dissatisfaction becomes active demand.

### Example

A manager has complained about scheduling for three years.

They finally buy software after:

* opening a second office;
* hiring ten workers;
* losing an important client;
* introducing regulatory time tracking.

### Implication for interviewing

Whenever adoption happened, ask:

Why then?

### Implication for opportunity discovery

Trigger circumstances can become:

* qualification signals;
* segmentation variables;
* sales triggers;
* onboarding moments.

This bridges discovery and commercial strategy.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

# 17\. Jobs Are Not Personas

**[Christensen — high confidence conceptually]**

### Explanation

Personas describe people.

Jobs explain behavior in context.

A persona might say:

Stefan, 47, owns a construction company, manages 22 employees, prefers mobile apps.

A job description might say:

When several active projects change at short notice, he needs to re-coordinate workers quickly without individually contacting everyone.

The second is much closer to explaining product adoption.

### Example

Two owners fitting the same persona may behave completely differently because their businesses operate under different circumstances.

### Implication for interviewing

Persona attributes should never substitute for interviewing around real events.

### Implication for opportunity discovery

Use personas or ICPs as indexing metadata around jobs, not as the fundamental unit of discovery.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

# 18\. Jobs Should Explain Observable Choice

**[Christensen — high confidence]**

### Explanation

A useful job theory should help explain why customers:

* chose one solution;
* rejected another;
* tolerated certain tradeoffs;
* switched;
* stayed with an imperfect solution.

If your formulation explains everything equally well, it probably explains nothing.

For example:

"People want to be productive"

is far too broad.

### Example

Better:

When tomorrow's staffing plan changes after office hours, operations managers need to reassign workers without individually contacting the entire team.

That formulation predicts certain behaviors and competitors.

### Implication for interviewing

Test candidate job formulations against actual stories.

Ask:

Does this explain why this person behaved this way?

### Implication for opportunity discovery

Reject overly generic jobs.

A useful job must be specific enough to define:

* circumstances;
* desired progress;
* existing alternatives;
* observable struggle.

# Important Conceptual Boundary: Job vs Need vs Task vs Solution

This distinction is worth preserving even though exact terminology differs across JTBD schools.

### Task

Send tomorrow's schedule.

Too narrow.

### Need

Better coordination.

Too abstract.

### Solution

Scheduling app.

Supplier framing.

### Job / desired progress

When plans change late in the day, reorganize tomorrow's crews quickly enough that everyone knows where they need to go.

This is usually the most useful discovery framing.

The harness should favor the fourth formulation without becoming doctrinaire about terminology.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

# What *Competing Against Luck* Adds Beyond Generic Customer Discovery

The core contribution is not:

Ask customers better questions.

Books such as *The Mom Test*, Portigal, and *Deploy Empathy* cover that better.

The contribution is:

**Interpret customer behavior through a causal theory of progress.**

The strongest principles worth importing are therefore:

**Circumstance → desired progress → struggle → currently hired solution → tradeoffs → switching behavior.**

That structure gives the interviewer a conceptual model for deciding **what evidence means**.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

# Competing Against Luck vs. Jim Kalbach's JTBD Playbook

These two should occupy different layers in your harness.

|                        | **Competing Against Luck**                   | **The Jobs To Be Done Playbook — Kalbach**            |
| ---------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Primary value          | Mental model                                 | Operational framework                                 |
| Core question          | Why do customers make the choices they make? | How do we systematically identify and structure jobs? |
| Job framing            | Progress in circumstances                    | Structured job descriptions and mappings              |
| Strongest contribution | Causality                                    | Research operationalization                           |
| Competition            | Solutions hired for the same job             | Can be mapped systematically around jobs              |
| Interview value        | Tells you what causal story to investigate   | Gives more concrete methods for capturing jobs        |
| Outputs                | Theory of customer choice                    | Job statements, maps, outcomes, hierarchies           |
| Best use in harness    | Reasoning layer                              | Representation and synthesis layer                    |

A useful way to think about them:

**Christensen tells the harness what a job means.** **Kalbach tells the harness what to do with one after finding it.**

You should therefore avoid duplicating Kalbach's:

* job statement templates;
* job hierarchies;
* job mapping procedures;
* desired-outcome structures;
* systematic job decomposition.

Use Christensen upstream as the epistemic model.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

# Where the Two Frameworks Slightly Differ in Emphasis

Kalbach's framework tends to make JTBD easier to represent as structured research data.

That is valuable, but there is a danger:

Once the methodology becomes structured, researchers can start treating jobs as objects they need to **categorize** rather than causal theories they need to **explain**.

Christensen provides a useful corrective.

The harness should not decide:

"We've filled the Job field, therefore we've identified the job."

Instead it should demand evidence linking:

**circumstance → motivation → behavior → selection → progress.**

If that causal chain is missing, the "job" is still mostly a hypothesis.

# Concepts That Should NOT Become Separate Harness Rules

Several Christensen ideas strongly overlap with other books you're already incorporating.

### Avoid asking customers what they want

Covered better by *The Mom Test*.

### Look at actual behavior

Covered better by Fitzpatrick, Hansen, Portigal, and Torres.

### Ask for specific stories

Covered much more operationally by Torres and Hansen.

### Investigate alternatives

Already heavily represented in Kalbach and founder-led sales.

### Look for workarounds

Covered repeatedly in discovery literature.

### Avoid demographic-only segmentation

Worth keeping as a JTBD mental model, but not as dozens of implementation rules.

So I would **not** import *Competing Against Luck* as a large rule set. It should contribute a small number of high-level reasoning constraints.

# JTBD Mental Models Worth Preserving in the Harness

I would preserve these **nine**, and push almost everything else downstream to Kalbach or the interview-method books.

### 1\. Progress, Not Product

Interpret demand as progress someone is trying to make, not demand for a particular feature or product.

**Harness rule:**

Never convert a customer problem directly into a solution requirement. First represent the desired progress independently of the solution.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

### 2\. Circumstance Creates the Job

A job only becomes intelligible within the situation in which it occurs.

**Harness rule:**

A job claim without relevant circumstances is incomplete.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

### 3\. Search for Causal Stories

The purpose of interviewing is not merely discovering correlations between customer characteristics and problems.

**Harness rule:**

Seek the causal chain explaining why behavior occurred.

Preferred structure:

**circumstance → struggle → action → solution choice → outcome**

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

### 4\. Study Hiring and Firing

Adoption and abandonment are especially valuable evidence.

**Harness rule:**

Whenever a customer started, stopped, replaced, or seriously considered replacing a solution, reconstruct the switching event.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

### 5\. Competition Is Job-Defined

Products compete when customers hire them for the same progress.

**Harness rule:**

Discover competitors from customer behavior rather than from software categories.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

### 6\. Demographics Are Context, Not Explanation

Firmographics can help locate customers but rarely explain demand by themselves.

**Harness rule:**

Do not infer jobs from industry, company size, title, or demographics without behavioral evidence.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

### 7\. Separate Stable Job From Temporary Solution

Technology changes faster than underlying customer progress.

**Harness rule:**

Express the opportunity in a way that remains meaningful even if today's tools disappear.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

### 8\. Capture Functional + Emotional + Social Consequences When They Matter

Customers rarely optimize only for functional efficiency.

**Harness rule:**

When emotional or social consequences appear causally important, retain them as dimensions of the job rather than dismissing them as noise.

Do **not** force every job into all three dimensions.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

### 9\. "Why Now?" Reveals Activation Conditions

A problem can exist for years without producing demand.

**Harness rule:**

Distinguish the existence of a problem from the circumstances that make someone act on it.

This is particularly important for your harness because it connects **discovery → qualification → eventual sales**.

\-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 

## Recommended role in your overall methodology

I would place *Competing Against Luck* above Kalbach in the conceptual stack:

**Christensen** → *What phenomenon are we trying to understand?*

**Kalbach** → *How do we describe and structure the jobs we discover?*

**Torres / Hansen / Portigal** → *How do we get trustworthy stories revealing those jobs?*

**Fitzpatrick** → *How do we avoid contaminating the evidence?*

**Testing Business Ideas** → *How do we test whether the inferred opportunity is commercially real?*

**Founding Sales** → *How do we determine whether that problem turns into an actual buying process?*

For your harness, that separation is important. *Competing Against Luck* should be a **small but high-authority reasoning layer**, not another large collection of interview scripts.
